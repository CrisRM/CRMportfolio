Accessibility transformation

A customer who cannot use our products with a screen reader, a keyboard or magnification cannot book a ticket or find the information they need. I led the accessibility strategy for ATG's digital products. This case covers how we moved from occasional audits to a standing programme across the web, apps and content: one standard, a remediation plan ordered by user impact, clear decisions on the hardest components, measurement, and a hand-over that stops open items from being lost.

Goals

What the work had to achieve

Around 16 million people in the UK and about 30% of US adults have a disability. Older customers, including high spenders, meet many of the same barriers.

Business goals

  • Let every customer complete checkout independently.
  • Protect conversion and revenue while the fixes ship.
  • Meet US and EU expectations before the launch in Germany, Spain and Austria.
  • Build accessibility into delivery so it stops being a retrofit.

Customer goals

  • Find a show and a date with a keyboard or screen reader.
  • Choose seats and read price and availability without relying on colour.
  • Zoom and enlarge text without losing a function.
  • Find access information early and reach a person when needed.

Context and evidence

Where it started and where it stands

The programme builds on earlier reviews. Each one changed what we prioritised.

May 2022

UK access review

Experts with lived experience tested ATG Tickets against WCAG 2.1, including a blind screen reader user on video. Findings included sideways scrolling on mobile, a seating plan that relied on colour, weak alt text, "click here" links, a carousel that could not be stopped, and access information buried in long text. This review is the baseline the programme builds on.

2024

US venue review

A screen reader review of the Kings Theatre and Broadway in Detroit ticket journeys against WCAG 2.1. An expert screen reader user could browse shows but could not select tickets. The seat layout could not be used by keyboard alone, and buying a wheelchair space was not possible.

Oct to Nov 2025

End-to-end audit for the US launch

An expert audit of atgtickets.com, end to end, with assistive technology. The checkout round found 59 issues, rated by user impact from Violation 1 (low) to Barrier 5 (severe). Two were severe and 12 were very high. An accessibility statement and contact route were published for launch.

Mar to Jun 2026

Checkout remediation

Fixes tracked in Jira and worked through in priority order, with every deferral logged with its rationale, alternatives, risk and owner.

Sep 2026

Audit of standalone and subscription flows

An expert audit of 12 pages found 73 issues: 14 low, 30 medium, 25 high and 4 very high. The most common failures were name, role and value (29) and information and relationships (23). These are mostly build-quality issues, which is why the programme moved to acceptance criteria and automated checks.

Approach

Six decisions that shaped the programme

One standard, tested in layers

WCAG 2.2 AA is the target for UK, US and EU checkout. Automated scanning runs in QA. Manual testing with assistive technology and annual expert reviews cover the rest. Each fix is reviewed by the developer, checked by the test engineer against acceptance criteria, then tested by the squad.

An audit is a baseline

Finishing every audit finding does not make a product compliant. We recommended an independent specialist review before anyone makes a formal compliance claim.

Fix at source, not with an overlay

We compared two widget vendors on monitoring, automated fixes, help desk and support. A widget went live to give customers assistive options while we fixed the code, and we did not rely on it for core issues.

Order work by user impact

Anything that stops a customer finishing a purchase comes first. Cosmetic items come last.

Write down what you do not fix

Deferred items go in a decision log with the reason, the alternatives and the risk, so they are not rediscovered as surprises.

Make it part of normal delivery

Accessibility checks sit in ticket acceptance criteria. Test engineers add automated tests to checkout so a minimum level holds from release to release.

Deep dive

The seatmap

The seatmap was the hardest part of checkout. It is an interactive SVG map, so standard HTML semantics do not apply. Roles, names and keyboard behaviour had to be designed and built.

Keyboard model

An engineering spike in March 2026 settled the navigation. Seats are grouped by level and row and ordered by coordinates, so one set of rules works for every UK and US venue without hardcoded values.

Tab: next section or row Arrow keys: next seat Enter / Space: select Esc: go back

What shipped

  • Seats exposed as buttons with accessible names
  • Grid, row and cell structure for the map
  • Visible focus and live-region announcements
  • A modal for ticket selection with a focus trap
  • Keyboard access to "View from seat", zoom and date controls

The colour problem

Price bands get their colours from a fixed palette, and the colours move down the palette when top prices sell out. The 2022 review had already advised shape as well as colour. A full visual fix means a pattern, shape or label on every seat in every venue configuration, which belongs in the seatmap redesign.

In the meantime, seats announce price and availability to screen readers, and the colours were tested to a 3:1 contrast ratio. A diagonal stripe and a checkmark are lined up for user testing.

Zoom at 200% and above

At 200% zoom the site switches to the mobile layout, which has no date arrows. The back button still works as an alternative path. Redesigning every breakpoint from 200% to 400% for one edge case was disproportionate for the current map, so the item moved to the new seatmap.

Measuring impact

How we know it is working

We tracked clicks on the accessibility widget, on the hypothesis that clicks fall as the underlying issues are fixed. The data is cached and refreshed periodically, so it is a signal and not an exact count. From April to May 2026, UK clicks held steady and US clicks fell gradually.

Success measures

  • Customers complete checkout with assistive tools, confirmed by tested scenarios.
  • Fewer contact centre and box office reports of being unable to check out online.
  • Checkout conversion holds during the updates.
  • 100% of audit-identified A and AA issues resolved.
  • Less reliance on the widget.

Process measures

  • Accessibility checks in ticket acceptance criteria.
  • Automated accessibility tests running in the checkout pipeline.
  • An annual manual review and an up-to-date accessibility statement.

Beyond checkout

Accessibility across the product

Native app

Ten iOS findings against WCAG A and AA sit at the lowest backlog priority while they gate the German and Spanish launches. My app use case sets a target of zero before EU launch and recommends raising their priority and running the same audit on Android and the web views.

Tickets, transfer and basket

Each use case carries WCAG 2.2 AA as a requirement: contrast, touch targets, VoiceOver and TalkBack labels, large-text-safe cards, a level-two heading for the order summary, and live announcements for totals, errors and the basket timer.

Content design

The content guidelines set a reading age of 11 to 12, a proper heading structure, short sentences, plain language and sentence case. They are adapted for German, Austrian and Spanish copy.

Design system

The digital ticket is a design-system component with WCAG 2.2 AA and large-text safety as requirements, so each fix is made once and every product team inherits it.

Open items and next steps

What remains

I would rather show the open work than hide it.

  • Commission an independent specialist before any formal compliance claim.
  • Run an end-to-end VoiceOver key navigation review of checkout. Most testing so far used the Tab key.
  • Assign colour-only pricing and 200% zoom to the new seatmap as named requirements with an owner.
  • Audit the voucher and upsell screens, which earlier audits did not cover.
  • Finish the remaining medium and low findings, then request a re-audit.
  • Raise the priority of the iOS findings and audit Android.

Lessons

What I would repeat

Name accessibility requirements before the build starts. Most of the cost on the seatmap came from retrofitting a component that was designed without them.

Keep the decision log. It turned arguments about what was done into a record of what was chosen and why.

Treat audits as a starting list, and measure the programme by whether customers can buy a ticket.

Localisation

As ATG Tickets moved from the UK and US into Germany, Austria and Spain, copy that was translated word for word would have sounded foreign and cost customers' trust. I established a localisation process for content design: one set of principles, and a content design guide written for each market with its own address form, register and terminology, so the product keeps one voice and reads naturally in each region.

The problem

Translation is not localisation

ATG's content design guide was written in English for UK and US customers. Launching in Europe raised questions that a translation cannot answer. Do customers expect to be addressed formally or informally? Which word for a ticket sounds right at a theatre? How is a price, a date or a seat written? How should an error message sound when someone is paying?

The aim was to answer those questions once, per market, and write them down so designers, content writers, engineers and agencies make the same choices.

Approach

One source guide, adapted for each market

Each market guide follows the same structure, so a change in one can be compared with the others.

What stays the same

  • Three principles: human, clear and experience-led.
  • The tone table: proud, helpful, simple and transparent, never pushy, vague or generic.
  • Persuade, never pressure. We let a premium product speak for itself and offer extras without pushing.
  • Patterns for errors, info boxes, warnings, confirmations, buttons, fields, empty states and notifications.
  • Accessibility and inclusive language, and a glossary of ATG terms.

What changes in each market

  • Address form and when the tone becomes more formal.
  • Vocabulary and the local terms for theatre and ticketing.
  • Spelling norms and how dates, times, numbers and prices are written.
  • Reading level, sentence style and how inclusive language is handled.
  • Examples drawn from real ATG screens in that language.

Deep dive

Austria: testing the German guide against the market

Austria shares a language with Germany but not its conventions, so I did not reuse the German guide. I benchmarked five Austrian theatre and ticketing platforms and used the findings to adapt it.

Address form

Every Austrian-owned platform uses the formal "Sie" throughout, including in warm and inviting copy. The one exception is an international brand that uses the informal "du" as a deliberate brand decision. The Austrian guide uses "Sie", unlike the German one.

Two words for a ticket

"Karte" is the institutional, premium term, and "Ticket" is the neutral, transactional one. Both are correct Austrian usage. The finding challenged a strict do and don't. "Karte" suits a premium brand, but banning "Ticket" in technical contexts such as transfer does not reflect the market, so it went to the team as a decision.

Showing what changed

The Austrian guide is colour-coded against the German version. One colour marks content changed for the market, one marks extra localisation context, and one marks the switch from "du" to "Sie" and the Austrian terms. Reviewers can see each decision and challenge it.

Process

How the guides were made and checked

1

Start from the source guide

The UK and US content design guide supplies the structure, principles and UI patterns.

2

Adapt, with real examples

Each guide is rewritten in its language, with examples from ATG screens in that language and a glossary of do's and don'ts for local terms.

3

Review with native speakers

Drafts went through several versions with comments from native-speaking colleagues in each market before the final guides.

4

Benchmark where the market is unclear

For Austria, a competitor analysis settled the address form and terminology questions with evidence, and left an open question for the team on "Karte" and "Ticket".

5

Put the guide to work

The UK and US tone of voice is packaged as a reusable AI skill that drafts interface copy for a region and checks it for generic AI-sounding writing before it ships.

Lessons

What I would repeat

Do not assume a shared language means a shared voice. Germany and Austria needed different answers to the first question in the guide, how to address the customer.

Keep the principles global and let the details be local. The shared structure is what lets one team maintain several markets.

Look at what the market does before deciding. Evidence from live platforms turned a matter of opinion into a question of register.

I led the accessibility strategy, and this case covers how those audits became a programme: a single standard, a way of scoping and rating audits, defined roles, lines on the product roadmap, and measures that show progress. The problem

Audits without a system around them

Reviews were commissioned per site and per market. The UK was reviewed in 2022, two US venue sites in 2024, atgtickets.com end to end in 2025, and standalone and subscription flows in 2026. Each review produced a long list of findings.

The same issue shows how this played out. The 2022 review said the seating plan relied on colour. In 2026 the colour-only price indicators were still an open item, deferred to the next seatmap.

The programme needed five things: a standard, a way to scope and rate audits, owners for each type of work, a place on the roadmap, and measures.

Scope by journey and priority

The main audit followed the customer journey end to end on atgtickets.com. Audit page lists were split into phases. Browse and venue pages came first, the seatmap second, and extras and payment third. For the US venue sites, each page had a priority from 1 to 3. For the 2026 audit, every page listed the user actions the auditor needed to perform, so deep states such as a booking with extras could be reached.

One severity scale

Findings were rated by what they do to a customer, from Violation 1 (low) to Barrier 5 (severe). The scale decided the order of work. Anything that stopped a purchase went first.

Providers compared on the job

We compared managed audit and support providers, including one that connects its issue tracker to Jira, and two widget vendors. The decision was expert audits to diagnose, a widget as an interim aid, and fixes made in the product code.

Our own reviews

Accessibility is a weighted dimension, at 10%, in the Product Health Check I use to review flows. The basic checks are colour contrast, keyboard navigation, screen reader labels and touch targets.

Accessibility in design

Built into UI delivery

Audits find problems after the build. I introduced accessibility into design so that fewer problems reach the build. It sits in two places: the design system and the solution handover.

In the design system

Accessibility is part of how a component is defined. The digital ticket component carries WCAG 2.2 AA and large-text safety as requirements. Fixing an issue in the component means every team that uses it inherits the fix, which is why adopting the design system in the apps is on the roadmap as an accessibility enabler.

In solution handovers

Each handover carries accessibility as a written requirement and a test scenario, so delivery teams get both together:

  • Colour contrast and touch target size
  • VoiceOver and TalkBack labels
  • Large text on every screen
  • Access information easy to spot, for example on the ticket

Roadmaps

Getting accessibility onto the roadmap

Each development team owns its own roadmap. My job was to keep accessibility visible across all of them. It has its own lines on the product pipeline, and it travels inside projects that touch the same components.

Operating model

What keeps it running

Maintenance

  • Manual checks every year.
  • Compliance documentation kept up to date.
  • Vendor and platform compliance checked.
  • Legal requirements reviewed on a schedule.

Launch gating for the US

In November 2025 we separated what had to be ready at launch from what could follow. The accessibility statement, contact route and legal review were ready. Full WCAG 2.2 AA conformance and the maintenance documentation were in progress and tracked as not required for launch.

WCAG 2.2 AA is a stated requirement in the UX use cases for tickets, transfer, the basket and the app. Content guidelines set the reading age and heading rules. The project pages hold the plan, the decision log and the measures.

Measures

Customers able to check out with assistive tools, fewer reports of being unable to check out online, conversion held, all audit-found A and AA issues resolved, less use of the widget, and automated checks in the pipeline. The widget click trend fell in the US between April and May 2026.

Lessons

What I would do again, and what I would change

Do again

  • Rate every finding on one scale and let the scale set the order.
  • Log what you will not fix, with a named receiving team.
  • Put WCAG into use-case requirements, so designers meet it before build.
  • Attach accessibility to roadmap items that already have a date.
  • Audit every new product and take the findings to the team that owns it.

Change

  • Start automated tests alongside the first fixes, not after them.
  • Name the owner for each scope before the previous owner leaves.
  • Audit extras, vouchers and apps at the same time as checkout.
  • Raise app findings above the lowest priority when they gate a launch.
