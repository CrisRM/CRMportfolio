Accessibility transformation

A customer who cannot use our products with a screen reader, a keyboard or magnification cannot book a ticket or find the information they need. I led the accessibility strategy for ATG's digital products. This case covers how we moved from occasional audits to a standing programme across the web, apps and content: one standard, a remediation plan ordered by user impact, clear decisions on the hardest components, measurement, and a hand-over that stops open items from being lost.

Goals

What the work had to achieve

Around 16 million people in the UK and about 30% of US adults have a disability. Older customers, including high spenders, meet many of the same barriers.

Business goals

  • Let every customer complete checkout independently.
  • Protect conversion and revenue while the fixes ship.
  • Meet US and EU expectations before the launch in Germany, Spain and Austria.
  • Build accessibility into delivery so it stops being a retrofit.

Customer goals

  • Find a show and a date with a keyboard or screen reader.
  • Choose seats and read price and availability without relying on colour.
  • Zoom and enlarge text without losing a function.
  • Find access information early and reach a person when needed.

Context and evidence

Where it started and where it stands

The programme builds on earlier reviews. Each one changed what we prioritised.

May 2022

UK access review

Experts with lived experience tested ATG Tickets against WCAG 2.1, including a blind screen reader user on video. Findings included sideways scrolling on mobile, a seating plan that relied on colour, weak alt text, "click here" links, a carousel that could not be stopped, and access information buried in long text. This review is the baseline the programme builds on.

2024

US venue review

A screen reader review of the Kings Theatre and Broadway in Detroit ticket journeys against WCAG 2.1. An expert screen reader user could browse shows but could not select tickets. The seat layout could not be used by keyboard alone, and buying a wheelchair space was not possible.

Oct to Nov 2025

End-to-end audit for the US launch

An expert audit of atgtickets.com, end to end, with assistive technology. The checkout round found 59 issues, rated by user impact from Violation 1 (low) to Barrier 5 (severe). Two were severe and 12 were very high. An accessibility statement and contact route were published for launch.

Mar to Jun 2026

Checkout remediation

Fixes tracked in Jira and worked through in priority order, with every deferral logged with its rationale, alternatives, risk and owner.

Sep 2026

Audit of standalone and subscription flows

An expert audit of 12 pages found 73 issues: 14 low, 30 medium, 25 high and 4 very high. The most common failures were name, role and value (29) and information and relationships (23). These are mostly build-quality issues, which is why the programme moved to acceptance criteria and automated checks.

Approach

Six decisions that shaped the programme

One standard, tested in layers

WCAG 2.2 AA is the target for UK, US and EU checkout. Automated scanning runs in QA. Manual testing with assistive technology and annual expert reviews cover the rest. Each fix is reviewed by the developer, checked by the test engineer against acceptance criteria, then tested by the squad.

An audit is a baseline

Finishing every audit finding does not make a product compliant. We recommended an independent specialist review before anyone makes a formal compliance claim.

Fix at source, not with an overlay

We compared two widget vendors on monitoring, automated fixes, help desk and support. A widget went live to give customers assistive options while we fixed the code, and we did not rely on it for core issues.

Order work by user impact

Anything that stops a customer finishing a purchase comes first. Cosmetic items come last.

Write down what you do not fix

Deferred items go in a decision log with the reason, the alternatives and the risk, so they are not rediscovered as surprises.

Make it part of normal delivery

Accessibility checks sit in ticket acceptance criteria. Test engineers add automated tests to checkout so a minimum level holds from release to release.

Deep dive

The seatmap

The seatmap was the hardest part of checkout. It is an interactive SVG map, so standard HTML semantics do not apply. Roles, names and keyboard behaviour had to be designed and built.

Keyboard model

An engineering spike in March 2026 settled the navigation. Seats are grouped by level and row and ordered by coordinates, so one set of rules works for every UK and US venue without hardcoded values.

Tab: next section or row Arrow keys: next seat Enter / Space: select Esc: go back

What shipped

  • Seats exposed as buttons with accessible names
  • Grid, row and cell structure for the map
  • Visible focus and live-region announcements
  • A modal for ticket selection with a focus trap
  • Keyboard access to "View from seat", zoom and date controls

The colour problem

Price bands get their colours from a fixed palette, and the colours move down the palette when top prices sell out. The 2022 review had already advised shape as well as colour. A full visual fix means a pattern, shape or label on every seat in every venue configuration, which belongs in the seatmap redesign.

In the meantime, seats announce price and availability to screen readers, and the colours were tested to a 3:1 contrast ratio. A diagonal stripe and a checkmark are lined up for user testing.

Zoom at 200% and above

At 200% zoom the site switches to the mobile layout, which has no date arrows. The back button still works as an alternative path. Redesigning every breakpoint from 200% to 400% for one edge case was disproportionate for the current map, so the item moved to the new seatmap.

Measuring impact

How we know it is working

We tracked clicks on the accessibility widget, on the hypothesis that clicks fall as the underlying issues are fixed. The data is cached and refreshed periodically, so it is a signal and not an exact count. From April to May 2026, UK clicks held steady and US clicks fell gradually.

Success measures

  • Customers complete checkout with assistive tools, confirmed by tested scenarios.
  • Fewer contact centre and box office reports of being unable to check out online.
  • Checkout conversion holds during the updates.
  • 100% of audit-identified A and AA issues resolved.
  • Less reliance on the widget.

Process measures

  • Accessibility checks in ticket acceptance criteria.
  • Automated accessibility tests running in the checkout pipeline.
  • An annual manual review and an up-to-date accessibility statement.

Beyond checkout

Accessibility across the product

Native app

Ten iOS findings against WCAG A and AA sit at the lowest backlog priority while they gate the German and Spanish launches. My app use case sets a target of zero before EU launch and recommends raising their priority and running the same audit on Android and the web views.

Tickets, transfer and basket

Each use case carries WCAG 2.2 AA as a requirement: contrast, touch targets, VoiceOver and TalkBack labels, large-text-safe cards, a level-two heading for the order summary, and live announcements for totals, errors and the basket timer.

Content design

The content guidelines set a reading age of 11 to 12, a proper heading structure, short sentences, plain language and sentence case. They are adapted for German, Austrian and Spanish copy.

Design system

The digital ticket is a design-system component with WCAG 2.2 AA and large-text safety as requirements, so each fix is made once and every product team inherits it.

Open items and next steps

What remains

I would rather show the open work than hide it.

  • Commission an independent specialist before any formal compliance claim.
  • Run an end-to-end VoiceOver key navigation review of checkout. Most testing so far used the Tab key.
  • Assign colour-only pricing and 200% zoom to the new seatmap as named requirements with an owner.
  • Audit the voucher and upsell screens, which earlier audits did not cover.
  • Finish the remaining medium and low findings, then request a re-audit.
  • Raise the priority of the iOS findings and audit Android.

Lessons

What I would repeat

Name accessibility requirements before the build starts. Most of the cost on the seatmap came from retrofitting a component that was designed without them.

Keep the decision log. It turned arguments about what was done into a record of what was chosen and why.

Treat audits as a starting list, and measure the programme by whether customers can buy a ticket.

A theme builder and AI tools now cut the days or weeks of manual design work behind each producer-branded experience, and automate documentation, delivery and prototyping.

Starting point

An immature system under growing demand

The system started in 2019 and was little more than a pattern library. When engineering later chose Material UI as the base for Backdrop, the code library, everything in the design system had to be restructured around it. Designers and contractors then came and went, each inheriting a partly finished system with no real handover.

The pattern library was not scalable or robust enough for the needs of the digital products, or to theme ATG's checkout, so the business gave the green light for a design system in the summer of 2022.

Goals

What the system had to achieve

My objective was to turn the design system into shared infrastructure: a single foundation every product team adopts, that serves today's products and scales with the business as it adds platforms, markets and brands.

Business goals

  • Win speed to market for producer brands: take a brand from marketing site to branded checkout in a fraction of the days or weeks it took by hand.
  • Lower the cost of change: stop hardcoded values and one-off components from adding technical debt. Target: 100% of library changes reflected in the library.
  • Make design system capacity predictable and measurable. Target: 95%+ of work starts from a ticket, and 85–90% is answered within the published turnaround.
  • Let the platform grow into new markets and brands without rebuilding its foundations.

Product goals

  • Make the system the default for every team on web, iOS and Android. Target: 100% adoption.
  • Keep one source of truth for tokens, so a decision made once reaches every platform.
  • Build accessibility in by default: components that meet WCAG 2.1 Level AA across colour, type and states, including dark mode.
  • Move from a central team to distributed governance, with a guild and named platform owners. Target: close to zero decisions reopened after review.
  • Build quality in rather than inspecting it afterwards. Target: 80%+ of components pass visual QA first time, and 100% of handovers complete before build.

User goals

  • Designers and engineers: find the right component, token and guidance without asking, and build from a complete handover.
  • Designers and engineers: a clear, quick route to request a change or contribute one, instead of building a one-off.
  • Customers: one consistent, familiar experience across web, iOS and Android.
  • Customers: branded checkouts that stay clear, readable and usable by everyone, whichever producer's brand they carry.

1 · Ownership and governance

From a hybrid to a distributed model

Governance changed with the system. Two operating models, each fitted to the stage the system was in.

Model 1 · While building the system

Hybrid

A centralised core team kept the system consistent and robust while it was still being built, with a guild of designers and engineers from the product teams around it.

  • A core team maintained the system and paired with contributors to develop it further.
  • The guild brought ideas and requests from every product team, and members built their own product components.
  • One team held the vision, the priorities and the quality bar while foundations, tokens and components were unfinished.
  • The limit: the guild was informal, a Slack group without defined roles, responsibilities or cadence.

Model 2 · Once the system matured

Distributed

As the system stabilised and demand spread across platforms and brands, I wrote an engineering RFC to share ownership instead of routing everything through one team.

  • Named owners for each platform, and a guild with a defined role.
  • A clear route to contribute to and extend the system, so teams stop building one-off components while they wait for an unclear review.
  • Shared care of the components, the roadmap and the documentation.
  • The restructure of governance and documentation began in April 2026.

What stayed the same in both models

One way in

Every change, from a bug fix to a new component, follows the same loop, from a request through design, review and build to release. A Slack intake feeds Jira, with a full and a lite version for requests of different sizes.

Review gates

A design review checks Figma parity for variants, states and tokens. Visual regression runs across every story. The packages ship as separate versioned releases, and risky changes go out as prereleases first so product teams can try them.

Measures and education

Process measures include requests that start with a ticket, complete handoffs, first-pass visual QA and time to resolve escalations. A health check scorecard tracks adoption, time to qualify and capacity unlocked. Education for designers reduces reliance on the system team.

2 · Native apps

Bringing iOS and Android onto the system

The ATGtickets app was built from 2024 and launched in the UK in October 2025 and the US in December 2025. The system had to reach it without waiting to be finished.

An interim process

While the system was being formalised, app designers worked to a lightweight process. Every component in flight sat on a shared board. Designs used existing components, or were uncomponentised but fully tokenised. No new token names could be invented, because naming belongs to the system team. A design review was the gate before handover to engineers.

Debt made visible

A component that went to build without review was flagged as design debt, not hidden. A component counted as done only when it was built on both iOS and Android with tokens applied on both.

One token source

The same token sources are transformed once and delivered to every platform: a typed package for the web, a Swift package for iOS and a Gradle library for Android. The components themselves are built by hand on each platform, so they follow the web.

What this unlocked in the app

In 2026 the app work included design system theming, a design token migration, dark mode and Material 3 and iOS 26 updates.

3 · Token architecture

Re-architecting the tokens for scale

The tokens were first architected on the Material Design 3 model. By December 2025 that structure had a scalability problem, so the architecture was redesigned and migrated across web, iOS and Android.

Starting point

The Material Design 3 model

The system followed the Material Design 3 approach, with the structure close to the standard layout in Tokens Studio.

  • Primitive, semantic and component-level layers, with names following a fixed path of category, type, item, sub-item and state.
  • Colour varied by significance and interaction state, so a button, a chip and a calendar day were described the same way.
  • Tokens managed in Figma with Tokens Studio, transformed with Style Dictionary and kept in a repository.

December 2025

Where it stopped scaling

The structure worked for the ATG brand theme. It did not stretch to more demanding brands or to the native apps.

  • A high degree of repetition, and too many intermediate layers before a usable token, adding volume and complexity without adding value.
  • Rigid for branding, difficult to evolve and not ready to serve native use cases seamlessly.
  • The internal brand and other bespoke brands would have needed a large number of bespoke tokens, which would be harder to maintain and scale.

January to August 2026

The re-architecture

A token restructure workshop compared how established systems, such as Material 3 and Radix Themes, organise their tokens, and set the rules for the new structure.

  • Fewer layers: the component tier was not used in the re-tokenisation. Primitives are referenced only by semantic tokens, and components use semantic tokens directly, never primitives or brand tokens.
  • One structure for web and apps, without duplicating tokens across them.
  • Backwards compatibility for Backdrop and its consumers, and legacy debt removed along the way.

Outcome

A structure that grows without debt

The architecture supports base themes such as the ATG brand, and more bespoke brands, without extra layers or unnecessary bespoke tokens. Changes stay local, branding is easier to extend, and the system can grow without accumulating technical debt, which raises efficiency and shortens delivery. A mono-palette brand and a multi-palette brand both fit, in light and dark.

Delivery

Migrated across all three platforms

Token architecture ideation began in January 2026 and moved into migration delivery, completed in August 2026 for web, iOS and Android. A tracker follows token adoption across designs.

4 · Theme builder

From a producer's brand to a theme, with AI

Producers and venues run branded checkouts and landing pages. Each one needs a theme built from their own brand and marketing site.

How it worked by hand

A manual guide set out the steps: amend the colour palette, create a favicon, resize the header logo to a fixed height, apply the common tokens for the header, page title, info bar, show card, footer, cookie bar and calendar, set the calendar states, and choose up to two fonts. Light and dark modes and bespoke show themes were out of scope.

Why it had to change

  • It is hard to capture all the requirements for a theme.
  • Timelines range from three days to two weeks.
  • It needs manual design work, because the design direction does not match the semantic architecture.
  • Sign-off by the product manager and the producer adds more rounds.

A hackathon with the design system team set the direction. The answer was a self-serve flow: take what the producer or their marketing agency already has, which is a logo, colours and fonts, and let AI do the translation into the token structure, with checks at the points where judgement matters.

1

Gather the brand assets

Logo, colours and fonts come from the producer or their marketing team, taken from their site and brand. Iconography and photography are optional. If the assets fail a check on colour or font accessibility, they go back to the stakeholder.

2

Prompt AI to build the theme

The assets and requirements go into an AI project set up around the system's semantic structure and key screens, with a shade and tint generator among the tools planned for it. A regional flow or an alternative reference can be added when the brief needs one.

3Gate · accessibility

Check accessibility first

AI reviews the theme for contrast and legibility. Any issue goes back to the designer to adjust the theme before it moves on.

4Gate · system review

Design system review

The design system team reviews the theme against the system's standards. If it is not approved, the designer iterates.

5Gate · sign-off

Try it, then sign off

A theme tester shows the theme on key screens, so the producer and the product manager approve something they have seen. Changes loop back to the theme.

6

Export and hand over

The approved theme is exported as JSON and handed to engineering.

The tool is a roadmap initiative to close the gap between designing a theme, getting it signed off and implementing it. Version 1.0 is in progress. The wider project brief treats a theme as brand plus configuration, with access controls and a live preview, and aims to take Figma out of the loop over time.

Lessons

What I would repeat

Start with how change enters the system. A process gave the team and its stakeholders something to point to when priorities competed.

Structure tokens around meaning. Once the semantic layer was right, brands, modes and platforms became inputs, and AI had something precise to work against.

Use AI where the work is rule-based and slow, and keep people where judgement and accountability sit.

Stage 1 · Brand

Adapting the brand to digital products

The ATG corporate brand guidelines set a tone of voice for internal and external communication, written for a company moving from UK theatre to international live entertainment. The rebrand, from red to purple, brought ATG's many visual brands together under one identity.

The first step was to adapt the new brand to digital products. The proposal was a sub-brand for Digital, derived from the corporate brand, with its own purpose and personality.

A brand strategy workshop defined the vision and purpose, the values, the personality and the voice. Teams voted on brand characteristics, wrote the headline they would want to read about the product in five years, and tested the personality against brand archetypes. A premium experience, quality of service, a human touch and connection came out as the core of the personality.

The brand was then translated into the interface, one building block at a time, and then built into the design system:

  • Typography. A type scale defined for the app and for the web.
  • Colour. The purple palette, with light tints for highlights and a dark mode.
  • Illustration. Principles for a clean, modern and bold style, not busy, with a delightful companion role.
  • Shape, motion and imagery. Shape language, motion and photography guidelines, so the brand reads the same on every screen.

Stage 2 · Voice

Content design, from principles to patterns

Content was the second step. Digital products still had no voice of their own: the copy was written by many hands, and the corporate guidelines did not cover interface copy. That gap is where the tone of voice began. A content designer was hired to turn the sub-brand into something a team can write with.

The brief

  • Audit the content across the native app, the web flows inside it, and CRM and push messages.
  • Fix text structure, such as alignment and paragraph length.
  • Localise for the UK and US.
  • Write a style guide so tone of voice can be applied with ease and accuracy.

What the guide fixes in place

  • Principles: human, clear and experiential.
  • Tone: proud, helpful, simple and transparent, never salesy, pushy or vague.
  • Ground rules: plain language for a reading age of 11 to 12, sentence case, mobile first.
  • Patterns: errors, info boxes, warnings, confirmations, buttons, fields, empty states and notifications.
  • Terminology: a glossary of ATG terms with do's and don'ts.

Words with a job to do

"Please" appears only in error and validation messages. "Thank you" follows a purchase. "Sorry" is kept for technical payment problems, so that when it appears, it signals something serious. Payment and legal moments get a more formal tone, because clarity matters most there.

Persuade, never pressure

The product speaks for itself. Extras and upgrades are offered with a benefit and without pressure, which keeps hospitality language warm and the checkout trustworthy.

Deep dive

Austria: testing the German guide against the market

Austria shares a language with Germany but not its conventions, so the German guide was not reused as it stood. A benchmark of five Austrian theatre and ticketing platforms shaped the adaptation.

Address form

Every Austrian-owned platform uses the formal "Sie" throughout, including in warm and inviting copy. The one exception is an international brand that uses the informal "du" as a deliberate brand decision. The Austrian guide uses "Sie", unlike the German one.

Two words for a ticket

"Karte" is the institutional, premium term, and "Ticket" is the neutral, transactional one. Both are correct Austrian usage. The finding challenged a strict do and don't, so it went to the team as a decision instead of a rule.

Showing what changed

The Austrian guide is colour-coded against the German version. One colour marks content changed for the market, one marks extra localisation context, and one marks the switch from "du" to "Sie" and the Austrian terms. Reviewers can see each decision and challenge it.

Stage 4 · Scale

An AI translation flow with human QA

Once native copywriters had adapted the voice for each region, the next need was a repeatable flow that keeps that persona consistent as markets multiply. It keeps the speed of AI and puts people where judgement matters: accuracy, register and tone.

Content design1

Write the English source

Copy is written once in Tolgee, the content management system, against the UK and US guide, with the principles and tone applied before anything is translated. Tolgee is the source of truth.

AI2

Translate with AI

Claude, connected to Tolgee, pulls the source copy. Each market's guide and glossary go in as instructions, so the output follows that market's address form, register and terminology.

Human QA3

Review by native speakers

Local reviewers check accuracy, tone and terminology, and the agreed changes feed back into the glossary.

Legal4

Legal review

Legal reviews the copy in every market, the UK, the US and Europe, before it goes to design.

Design5

Review in context

The same Tolgee copy is pulled into Figma, so length, layout and flow are checked on the real screen and the UI does not break.

Research6

Test where it matters

Content is tested with local customers in the markets that need it, starting with Austria.

Engineering7

Ship and sync

Translated strings are integrated into the code from the same source, followed by a final review.

The UK and US tone of voice is also packaged as a reusable AI skill. It drafts interface copy for a region and checks it for generic, AI-sounding writing before it ships.

Where it stands

Status and next steps

In place

  • Digital sub-brand and tone of voice.
  • A content design guide for the UK, US, Germany, Austria and Spain.
  • An AI translation flow with human QA, and an AI skill for regional copy.

Next

  • Launch markets in sequence, with customer insight streams per market.
  • Measure how customers rate clarity and ease in each language, once each market is live.
  • Extend the process to further markets with the same steps.

Lessons

What carries over to the next market

A shared language does not mean a shared voice. Germany and Austria needed different answers to the first question in the guide: how to address the customer.

Keep the principles global and let the details be local. The shared structure is what lets one team maintain several markets.

Use AI for speed and people for judgement. A fixed review step turns a draft translation into one that sounds native.

Stage 2 · Take stock

A retro before a roadmap

The team was asked one question: how well are we using AI to improve process efficiency in our design workflow? Seventy-nine notes were sorted into four groups.

How it is used

  • Research and data synthesis: transcripts, surveys and several Miro boards on one topic.
  • Code prototypes and rough layouts to take into Figma.
  • Mockup images, theme and colour tests, and icon sets for the design system.
  • Translation and regional content, and polished dev handover.
  • Seatmap SVGs that unblock engineers.

What works

  • Faster time to insight, and synthesis of research that used to be manual.
  • Prototypes in real code with complex interactions.
  • Coded visual design within reach of non-technical designers.
  • Quick sketching, and a way to get unstuck.

What does not

  • Results that are not always reliable, including hallucinated data, and no quality control standards.
  • Heavy steering and reprompting, and a gap between a prototype and the engineering stack.
  • Endless output, so that AI is needed to digest the output of AI.
  • Documents that do not say whether they are final or a "first Claude draft".
  • A design system that Claude interprets loosely.

What the team asked for

  • Quality control before AI-generated work is shared.
  • The design system connected fully to Claude, with coded components.
  • Fixed tools and processes for recurring tasks, using skills as well as connectors.
  • A full AI design workflow that becomes the norm.

The critique stayed in the plan. The three largest clusters, research, prototyping and the design system, became the three working groups of the hackathon.

Stage 3 · Build

Hackathon working groups

Each group took one part of the design lifecycle and chose one or two ideas to build in the sprint.

Research and usability testing

A project brief tool that starts from a PRD. It asks follow-up questions about the audience, the timeframe, the decisions the team needs to make, competitors and technical limits, then recommends a method and feeds a roadmap for the project.

Design system and visual design

The theme builder, which lets a producer's logo, colours and fonts be translated into the token structure by AI, with checks where judgement matters. It is described in the design systems case.

Wireframing, prototyping and ideation

The group worked out a method for prototypes that stakeholders can click and customers can test.

Prompt1

Start from a PRD

A well organised PRD, written with Claude, gives better output. Say upfront where the prototype will live and that it is a hosted, multi-page prototype.

Wireframe2

Ideate the idea

Wireframe with Claude Design, then ideate components before full pages.

Brand3

Style it in ATG's brand

The Figma connector brings in existing designs. Styling was more accurate from the live site than from the design system, which raised the question of whether the system needed better setup.

Code4

Code it simply

Ask for simple HTML, CSS and JavaScript instead of React, in a mobile frame of 390 by 844, and steer the output with more context.

Host5

Host and test

The code lives in GitHub and goes to a simple host, so the prototype can be shared and usability tested.

Prototyping speed rose by at least 50%, and research synthesis turnaround halved, from two weeks to one. Claude-powered research analysis also put insight into daily use, with research now cited in decisions at least daily.

Stage 4 · Product

An AI concierge, tested before launch

When AI moved into the product, the question was how much personality it should have. Two concepts for a concierge in the subscriptions renewal flow were tested with 24 customers across the UK and the US, on desktop and mobile web.

Concept 1: "ATG's Box Office, powered by AI"

Tech-focused, functional and unobtrusive. It invites the customer to start, and does not push.

Concept 2: "Theo at the Box Office"

Personified, prominent and conversational. A named character with warmth and a point of view.

8 / 12

said Theo stood out on the page, against 3 of 12 for the tech-focused concept

100%

17 / 24

The direction

  • Theo, with iterations: keep the purple button and floating toast for discoverability.
  • Label it clearly as AI, for example "Theo, your AI assistant", so that the name is not mistaken for a person.
  • Use the sparkle icon in place of the custom one.
  • Say upfront what it can help with, such as date, location, budget and genre, and what it cannot yet do, such as bookings and refunds.

What customers wanted instead

  • Suggested questions less centred on London and Broadway. Only 1 of 6 regional UK participants found them relevant.
  • Shows near me, last-minute or cheap seats, and shows by genre or for families.
  • The "AI can make mistakes" disclaimer kept.

Lessons

What carries over

Start from the process, not the tool. The retro found the gaps first, and the hackathon groups followed the themes it found.

Keep the critique in the room. Reliability, volume and unclear draft status were raised as loudly as the speed gains, and each one points to a control: quality checks before work is shared, a human review step in translation, and a clear label on anything AI-made.

Give the AI the context it needs. Output improved when the prompt said where the prototype would live, what the design system was and which voice to write in, and styling was more accurate from the live site than from the design system, which is a prompt to set the system up better for AI.

Name AI as AI, in the team and in the product. Customers were comfortable with a human name as long as the feature was clearly labelled.

Building the accessibility programme

Accessibility reviews at ATG started as separate audits for separate sites. I led the accessibility strategy, and this case covers how those audits became a programme: a single standard, a way of scoping and rating audits, defined roles, lines on the product roadmap, and measures that show progress. It is the operating model behind the checkout and seatmap case.

The problem

Audits without a system around them

Reviews were commissioned per site and per market. The UK was reviewed in 2022, two US venue sites in 2024, atgtickets.com end to end in 2025, and standalone and subscription flows in 2026. Each review produced a long list of findings.

The same issue shows how this played out. The 2022 review said the seating plan relied on colour. In 2026 the colour-only price indicators were still an open item, deferred to the next seatmap.

The programme needed five things: a standard, a way to scope and rate audits, owners for each type of work, a place on the roadmap, and measures.

Audits

How the audits were designed

Each audit had a different job. Together they cover people with lived experience, expert testing with assistive technology, automated scanning and our own design reviews.

Scope by journey and priority

The main audit followed the customer journey end to end on atgtickets.com. Audit page lists were split into phases. Browse and venue pages came first, the seatmap second, and extras and payment third. For the US venue sites, each page had a priority from 1 to 3. For the 2026 audit, every page listed the user actions the auditor needed to perform, so deep states such as a booking with extras could be reached.

One severity scale

Findings were rated by what they do to a customer, from Violation 1 (low) to Barrier 5 (severe). The scale decided the order of work. Anything that stopped a purchase went first.

Providers compared on the job

We compared managed audit and support providers, including one that connects its issue tracker to Jira, and two widget vendors. The decision was expert audits to diagnose, a widget as an interim aid, and fixes made in the product code.

Our own reviews

Accessibility is a weighted dimension, at 10%, in the Product Health Check I use to review flows. The basic checks are colour contrast, keyboard navigation, screen reader labels and touch targets.

Accessibility in design

Built into UI delivery

Audits find problems after the build. I introduced accessibility into design so that fewer problems reach the build. It sits in two places: the design system and the solution handover.

In the design system

Accessibility is part of how a component is defined. The digital ticket component carries WCAG 2.2 AA and large-text safety as requirements. Fixing an issue in the component means every team that uses it inherits the fix, which is why adopting the design system in the apps is on the roadmap as an accessibility enabler.

In solution handovers

Each handover carries accessibility as a written requirement and a test scenario, so delivery teams get both together:

  • Colour contrast and touch target size
  • VoiceOver and TalkBack labels
  • Large text on every screen
  • Access information easy to spot, for example on the ticket

Roadmaps

Getting accessibility onto the roadmap

Each development team owns its own roadmap. My job was to keep accessibility visible across all of them. It has its own lines on the product pipeline, and it travels inside projects that touch the same components.

How it was built

Five stages

2022 to 2024

Review

2025

Set the standard and the scope

For the US launch we adopted WCAG 2.2 AA, which has 55 success criteria. We published an accessibility statement and a contact route, added an interim widget, and commissioned an end-to-end expert audit of atgtickets.com.

Accessibility entered the design system and solution handovers. Checks entered ticket acceptance criteria, automated tests were planned for checkout, success measures were set, accessibility lines were added to the roadmap, and every new product is audited.

Extend

Apps, hospitality and the voucher and upsell screens come next, with an independent specialist review before any formal compliance claim.

Operating model

What keeps it running

Maintenance

  • Automated testing in QA.
  • Manual checks every year.
  • Compliance documentation kept up to date.
  • Vendor and platform compliance checked.
  • Legal requirements reviewed on a schedule.

Launch gating for the US

In November 2025 we separated what had to be ready at launch from what could follow. The accessibility statement, contact route and legal review were ready. Full WCAG 2.2 AA conformance and the maintenance documentation were in progress and tracked as not required for launch.

Where accessibility is written down

WCAG 2.2 AA is a stated requirement in the UX use cases for tickets, transfer, the basket and the app. Content guidelines set the reading age and heading rules. The project pages hold the plan, the decision log and the measures.

Measures

Customers able to check out with assistive tools, fewer reports of being unable to check out online, conversion held, all audit-found A and AA issues resolved, less use of the widget, and automated checks in the pipeline. The widget click trend fell in the US between April and May 2026.

Lessons

What I would do again, and what I would change

Do again

  • Rate every finding on one scale and let the scale set the order.
  • Log what you will not fix, with a named receiving team.
  • Put WCAG into use-case requirements, so designers meet it before build.
  • Attach accessibility to roadmap items that already have a date.
  • Audit every new product and take the findings to the team that owns it.

Change

  • Start automated tests alongside the first fixes, not after them.
  • Name the owner for each scope before the previous owner leaves.
  • Audit extras, vouchers and apps at the same time as checkout.
  • Raise app findings above the lowest priority when they gate a launch.
